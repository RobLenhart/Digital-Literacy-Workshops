# Speaker Notes: Spotting and Stopping Common Scams

These notes accompany the Scam Presentation PDF for this community workshop. 

---

## Slide 1: Spotting & Stopping Common Scams
**Key Takeaway:** Introduce the goal of the session - learning the warning signs of a scam and practical strategies on using technology safely, regardless of changing methods employed by scammers.

- **Introduction:** Welcome the audience and introduce yourself. Explain why we are hosting this session.
- **Core Goal:** Explain that we are focusing on spotting common warning signs so our strategies can be applied even as new scams continue to emerge.
- **Suggested Opening:** Hello! My name is (Your Name). Today, we are here to talk about spotting and stopping common scams. I hope that we can learn to identify common warning signs of a scam so that we can use the strategies we discuss today, even as the exact methods scammers use continue to evolve and change. If we follow the methods outlined here, we can all learn to use technology a little more safely and have a little more peace of mind.

---

## Slide 2: Why Scammers Target Us
**Key Takeaway:** Explain that being targeted by a scammer or falling for a scam isn't something to be ashamed of. Emphasize that these scams rely on psychology and only use technology as a delivery mechanism.

- **Anyone can be fooled / Even professionals will be tricked:** Mention that many companies run simulated phishing campaigns, basically "scamming" their own workers, and hold regular trainings on exactly what we are here to discuss today because even people who work around technology every day can have a lapse of judgement and open an attachment or click on a link that they shouldn't have. Being caught off guard doesn't mean someone is careless or unintelligent.
- **Scammers use psychology / Technology is just the vehicle:** Explain that these scams are about eliciting and emotional response like fear or trust, rather than relying on complications that emerge from a technology like email or text messaging. These technologies are just the delivery method, but the fundamentals of how scammers trick people haven't changed.
- **Identifying the scam is the hardest part / What do we look for?:** Once you know how to identify a scam when its happening, the actual protective steps are very straightforward. The biggest challenge is catching on to the scam early.

---

## Slide 3: Where Do Scams Happen?
**Key Takeaway:** Emphasize the technologies we will use as examples today. Explain again that these are just how the scams are delivered, but that we will quickly see a lot of overlap in the underlying methods regardless of what technology is used to contact us. 

---

## Slide 4: Phone Calls

- **Spoofed Numbers:** Explain that it is trivial to "spoof" a number, and that a scammer can disguise their phone number when placing calls as a way to make keeping track of them or blocking their phone numbers harder. 
- **What Claims Might They Make?:** Scammers will frequently pose as national bank chains or as the Social Security or Medicare office because these are easy ways to cast a wide net when placing dozens, hundreds, or even thousands of these calls. Eventually, someone they contact will have an account with Bank of America or be expecting a call about a missed Social Security Check. They may also pose as a specific family member if they have more targeted information on the individual they reach out to.
- **Voice Cloning / Increasingly common thanks to AI tools:** Because of emerging technologies, scammers can create voice changers to impersonate a particular person if their planning is granular enough. 

---

## Slide 5: Text Messages

- **What Will They Send You?:** A common scam performed via text messages will be fake tracking information from scammers posing as the USPS, UPS, FedEx, etc. These scams may also look like fundraisers, offers of free gifts, news, or seemingly random friendly conversation. 
- **Common to Include a Link to the Internet:** Many of the scams described above will include an external link to get you out of the text message and onto a website run by the scammers. Once you are on their site, you will likely be prompted for your personal or financial information while believing you are engaging with a legitimate party.

---

## Slide 6: Emails
**Key Takeaway:** Be thorough when explaining the mechanics of a "fake name" attached to an email address as well as how hyperlinks can mislead unsuspecting users and take them to fraudulent websites.

- **Fake Invoices / Password Reset Requests / Pose as Social Media Service or Retailer:** These are some of the most frequent methods used by scammers when reaching out to you via email. In all of these cases, their goal will, again, be to take you out of your email inbox and get you onto a website they run, and these sites will often be designed to look like big online retailers like Amazon or popular social media platforms like Facebook. Just like before, the goal here is to have you input sensitive information such as passwords or a credit card number.
- **Likely Sent Using a Fake "Name":** Many Email services will show you a "name" associated with the sender of the emails you receive, rather than the full email address the message was sent from. This makes it easy to know when you receive an email from someone with an address that doesn't clearly identify them, but it can also be abused if the scammer simply puts in "Facebook" or "Bank of America." Sometimes, these names will be filtered out but those filters can be bypassed by using abnormal variations of letters found in the name, such as the F with an extra line through it's bottom half as seen in the top picture on the slide.
- **Common to See Suspicious Links:** Scammers can also abuse "hyperlinks" in these emails. These hyperlinks are what allow normal emails to use phrases like "Click Here," and then send you to their legitimate website when you click on the word "here." However, these hyperlinks can also send you to sites that you may not expect, even when the address of the website is spelled out, like we see in the bottom picture on this slide. If you are unsure about the safety of a link, you can usually hover a mouse curser over the link to see the destination. On smart phones and other "touch" devices, you can also often HOLD your tap when you click on the link and an option along the lines of "copy URL" or "copy destination" should appear in the context menu.

## Slide 7: Social Media

- **Posing as a Friend or Family Member:** Scammers may create accounts that look exactly like real accounts and use these fake accounts when trying to scam friends or followers of the account they copied. They will usually use the same profile pictures and either copy names exactly or use very similar usernames to make distinguishing between the real and fake account difficult on first glance. 
- **Look for lack of "Mutuals" / Reach out through other channels:** If you are suspicious of a message you receive, check to make sure you are friends with or follow the sender. If they claim to be a new account, maybe because their original got hacked or they lost the password, check and see if other people you know are friends with them. You also should not hesitate to reach out through another channel to confirm the legitimacy of the account. Maybe give them a phone call and see if the story checks out.
- **Posing as a Friendly Stranger / Don't offer unearned trust:** Scammers may also use these fake accounts to reach out to as many strangers as they can in hopes of finding someone who will be receptive to friendly conversation with strangers. Sometimes this will look like romantic interest, but sometimes it will also just look like a fast made friendship. In either case, the scammer could be looking to get some kind of "dirt" they can eventually use to extort or blackmail their target, or they may just tell you they need a sum of money because they are in a tight spot.

## Slide 8: What Are Their Tactics
**Key Takeaway:** These are some of the primarily categories that the scams will fall into regardless of the exact methods used. This is where the underlying psychological element comes into the scam, and when we start to see how these scams are identical to scams that existed long before cell phones or the internet. 

- **Fear & Panic / Bill Collectors, Bank "Fraud":** Creating a sense of urgency or inducing fear and panic in someone can lead to them acting without fully thinking through their actions. If a scammer claims to be with the electric company and says they will shut off your power if you don't pay over the phone immediately, eventually they will find someone who really is worried about being behind on bills, and that person might bite. Similarly, if the scammer claims to be with Bank of America or Wells Fargo, or any other large chain of banks, and they tell their targets that there has been fraud on their account, but they need their account or debit card number immediately to stop the fraudulent transaction from going through, many people will panic and give away that precious information without taking steps to confirm that it really is their bank reaching out.
- **Greed / Lottery, Free Giveaways:** Scammers can also take advantage of targets who are looking for a big break. Telling a target that they won some special lottery or a big giveaway, but that they just need to give some kind of payment information to pay a shipping fee or cover the taxes will eventually catch someone who really believes they won something they don't even remembering entering into a drawing for.
- **Love / Family, Partnership:** Posing as a friend or family member is a shortcut to the kind of trust that many scammers rely on. When they can't pose as someone you know, they can create a new identity and try to earn your trust as someone who may or may not even exist.
- **Confusion / Medicare, Unusual Behavior:** Some scams rely on people not being able to navigate complicated systems. If someone gets a call saying they qualify for new Medicare benefits, they may become confused but ultimately give in because they don't know enough about those systems to keep up with whether or not those new benefits actually exist. The scammers may also employ confusing, unusual behaviors in hopes of drawing your attention elsewhere and performing a sort of digital "sleight of hand." 

---

## Slide 9: Fear & Panic

- **Bank "Fraud":** Many people will naturally panic if they believe someone has stolen money from their bank accounts. This sense of panic can make someone act irrationally
- **Bill Collections / IRS or Law Enforcement:** These are scams that rely on casting a wide net and eventually coming across someone who really is worried about being behind on taxes or having issues with law enforcement. These scams will usually demand payment in unusual ways to avoid chargebacks or being tracked. Remember, the IRS doesn't take Bitcoin, and they definitely don't take Visa gift cards.
- **Browser Pop-Ups / Flashing colors, "Your Computer is Infected":** These can oftentimes be the result of clicking on those suspicious links that were discussed earlier. The "infection" may or may not be real, but in these cases, the intention is to overwhelm the user and make them act quickly. There may be some kind of payment portal, where the pop-up or site claims that your computer can be "cleaned" if you just pay a ransom. Paying these ransoms is very rarely a good idea. In other cases, you might receive a follow up communication from someone posing as Tech Support, and this person will claim they can remove whatever is ailing your computer, but again, you will need to pay up. In this case, it is still inadvisable to actually pay these people, as they might just not fix anything once you've paid up, and they may even steal your payment information. 

---

## Slide 10: Love & Family

- **Impersonate Family Member:** If a scammer has specific information, they can pose as a friend or family member of their target. This could look like a request for bail money. Sometimes, it can take a darker turn if a scammer pretends to have kidnapped this family member and demands a ransom.
- **Romance Scams:** Scammers may assume false identities to lure in their targets. We usually call these "romance scams," but they can also take the shape of friendships. Sometimes the identities will just be fabricated using fake or stolen pictures. In other cases, the scammers will pretend to be a big celebrity. In 2025, one of these became a big news story when a woman divorced her husband and transferred $800,000 dollars to someone posing as Brad Pitt. The scammers convinced her that Brad wanted a serious relationship with her. I'm sorry to say, but Brad Pitt does not want to date you. 
- **Eventually They Need Money:** Whether they claim to be a family member going through a rough patch, a long distance lover who will come see you if you pay their airfare, or a celebrity who needs to borrow some money because their wealth is tied up in other investments, scams in this vein always end with a request, or demand, for your money. These requests will usually be accompanied by an insistence that the secrecy of any transaction is important. 

---

## Slide 11: Greedy & Generosity

- **You Won This Giveaway!:** Scammers can take advantage of someone believing they've just hit a big break. Maybe you won a special lottery and they promise you a large sum of money if you just pay the associated taxes. Maybe they say you entered a drawing for a new boat, but you have to pay a processing or delivery fee. If the scammer gets you excited enough, you might just think you really have some winnings coming your way and cough up a pittance compared to what they are promising you.
- **Won't You Donate to Our Cause?:** Unfortunately, as a flip-side to those giveaway scams, someone may pose as a charity or someone in desperate need to abuse the good nature of well meaning people. It can be difficult to know for sure if an individual saying they live in some war-torn corner of the world is really who they say they are, but scammers know they can eventually find people willing to donate to help out if they take advantage of these sad stories. Generally, the only advice here is to allow your generosity to be filtered through real charities or aid groups who have the network and capacity to find those in need and make sure the money is making it into the right hands. 

---

## Slide 12: Confusion

- **Posing as Medicare:** Systems like Medicare and Social Security are very complicated and scammers know they can take advantage of confusion that their would-be targets already have. Most people who use these systems won't be able to keep up with every new update to them, so when a stranger calls and says you might qualify for an extra $200 a month, you might be tempted to believe them. This could be a setup so they can get personal information from you and that could be leveraged for more targeted scams in the future, or even may be their first step in getting financial information from you. After all, if they are promising you extra money, they surely need to know what bank account to put it into. 
- **Fake Tech Support / Microsoft or Apple will never call you:** This is maybe the only example of a scam discussed today that entirely relies on someone not knowing enough about computers or the internet to understand when they are being scammed. The scammers can call you up and claim to be with Microsoft or Apple. They will say they needed to let you know that your computer has some virus they need to take care of immediately. It is very important to remember that these companies will NEVER call you directly just because you have a computer virus. 
- **Don't download strange programs:** Their goal is to have you download a program that is supposedly a part of their tech support. Sometimes these programs will be viruses themselves, but they are also often legitimate programs that the scammers will just abuse to take full advantage of your computer so they can steal information from you or install their viruses directly. Do not ever download a strange program because of what someone tells you in an email or a phone call. You can get an anti-virus program that will cover your computers and cell phones fairly affordably these days, and if you require tech support, you should go to a trusted person or business rather than accepting help from strangers on the internet.

## Slide 13: Red Flags
**Key Takeaway:** Emphasize that most legitimate issues will be resolved using US dollars. Scammers rely on these strange payment methods because it adds to the sense of confusion for their targets while also providing the funds through means that can be harder to block or trace. 

- **Demanding Payment in Unusual Ways:** Oftentimes, these scammers won't just want your credit or debit card information. Banks don't want to lose that money, so the financial institutions behind those cards are highly motivated to catch these scams as they happen and block unusual transactions. If your Visa is suddenly being charged across the country, let alone across the globe, they are very likely to flag and decline the charge.
- **Gift cards, Bitcoin, Wire transfer, Payment Apps:** Scammers are likely to say they need their payment through methods that are harder to track now. This could mean large sums in gift cards, or money sent via wire transfer or through payment apps like Zelle or Venmo. They will likely use accounts that they know will be flagged, but their plan is usually going to involve moving the money out of those accounts as quickly as possible. Because of how common Cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin have become, many scammers will even instruct their targets to find the closest "Bitcoin ATM," which will enable unknowing victims to turn their cash into these digital currencies and send them using simple confirmation codes.
- **Many retailers limit gift card purchases:** In the last decade or so, many retailers have actually begun to limit how many gift cards you can purchase in one transaction specifically because of these kinds of scams. Cashiers at those retailers will usually be trained to ask probing questions when someone is buying an unusually large value in gift cards in an effort to stop the scams then and there. Because of this, scammers may be more likely to demand money through other the means we discussed.

## Slide 14: Strategies
**Key Takeaway:** Focus on how losing connection with a scammer for a minute, calming down, thinking things through and talking to someone you trust can make all the difference. 

- **Stop and Breathe:** Scammers rely on a sense of panic and urgency. Having a few minutes to think through what they are telling you can be all it takes to realize something isn't right. This could mean realizing something is too good to be true, or taking a moment to talk to anyone else about what is happening and getting a second opinion from somebody you trust. 
- **Verify for Yourself:** It is fine to hang-up the phone if you aren't sure about the call. You can even tell a little white lie. Your real bank or family member will understand if you say the call got disconnected. Take out a bank statement or your debit card and find the phone number for your branch or the customer service line for your credit card. Call them back yourself so you know you're talking to the right people. If you call the bank back and they have no idea what you're talking about, you know then and there that a scammer was trying to trick you.  
- **Ask a Trusted Person:** Sometimes, you won't have somebody to call. In those cases, run things by somebody you know you can trust. If you have a friend or family member, they'll be much happier knowing they could help you BEFORE the scammers got your money or information. If you don't have somebody like that to check things over, I cannot recommend coming into your local library enough. Most librarians are very community oriented and would be more than happy to hear out what is going on and tell you if what you're saying makes sense or not.

## Slide 15: Staying Informed
**Key Takeaway:** Make sure the audience understands this hasn't been a list of every way a scammer may try to take advantage of them. Tactics are always changing and taking a few minutes to keep up with news in this field can put you in the driver's seat when they get that next suspicious phone call or email.

- **This Has Not Been Exhaustive:** Like we've been saying over and over, scammers are coming up with new strategies all the time. One I've come across in the last year or so is a QR code put up in a public parking space. The person I was with scanned the code and was directed to a site to pay for our parking. When they put in their credit card information, they ended up being charged way more than the couple of dollars they were expecting. This kind of scam would require a more local approach, but that doesn't make it any easier to avoid.
- **AARP Scams & Fraud:** The AARP has a page on their website that you can read through for free. They publish news stories and tips relating to these scams and even advertise a phone number you can dial to get in touch with their free "Fraud Watch Network Helpline."
- **Better Business Bureau:** The Better Business Bureau keeps a "Scam Tracker" page on their site. This features a live feed of scams reported to the BBB, along with the location and business name associated with the scam. You can also search for specific scams on there to see if something you're worried about has hit anyone in your area and get more information on how the scams work.
- **FTC Consumer Alerts:** The Federal Trade Commission also keeps a news feed on scams targeting consumers and offers advice on how to protect yourself from those scams. 
- **Closing Remarks** I hope you're feeling a little more informed after this. Using the strategies we've discussed here today along with the resources on this last slide, I hope you can all put your minds a little more at ease going forward and have confidence in your ability to identify a scammer the next time they try to reach out to you.
